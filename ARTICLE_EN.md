# Today It's Song Tao, Tomorrow It Could Be You — What the Dobot equity fight says about the options in your own pocket

> **Disclaimer**: All facts about Dobot here come from public reporting; Song Tao's allegations are his own claims, Dobot has publicly denied them, and the final word rests with regulators and the courts. This is industry commentary and general guidance — not legal or investment advice.

---

## 1. Today, it's Song Tao

In July 2026, a WeChat account called "Song Tao Robot" published a series of posts making a real-name complaint against Dobot (2432.HK, already listed in Hong Kong and then pursuing an A-share listing on China's ChiNext board) and its founder Liu Peichao.

Song Tao describes himself as a co-founder and former Executive Vice President / COO of Dobot. His central claim: the property share he should hold in the employee holding vehicle is **69.7373%**, but the prospectus records only **22.455%** — with roughly 47% of the share "unaccounted for."

Dobot quickly responded publicly, calling the allegations "untrue and seriously misleading."

The latest development: **on July 22, 2026, the Shenzhen Stock Exchange's listing review committee approved Dobot's ChiNext IPO** — the Greater Bay Area's first "H-to-A" case, just 86 days from acceptance to review. Song Tao's complaint did not change the listing; the equity dispute he alleges still awaits arbitration and the courts.

For ordinary people, that outcome is actually the most useful lesson: **your rights ultimately have to be asserted through hard evidence and proper procedure — not staked on any single moment.**

Who's right and who's wrong is for regulators and the law. What draws me to this is that, once you strip away the eye-catching labels — "co-founder," "hundreds of millions in equity," "IPO ambush" — the core is something very plain and very common:

**Someone wants to take his own equity, cleanly, before the company goes public — and finds out it isn't that easy.**

This has more to do with ordinary working people like you and me than you'd think.

---

## 2. Tomorrow, it could be you

You might say: he's a co-founder, a COO — that's a feud among big shots, what's it got to do with me, a coder?

Here's what: **if even the #2 co-founder can be locked out of his equity, what makes an ordinary engineer think he's safe?**

And this really isn't a one-off. Just over a month earlier, another former employee, Chen Hao, went public during Xiaohongshu's IPO run. His case already has a binding result: courts in Guangzhou found the company had unlawfully terminated his employment, and compensation for his lost options was awarded — with the courts affirming an important principle: **options are tied to the employment relationship and are an extension of labor compensation, not a "perk" the company can simply reclaim.**

One dispute still awaits arbitration and the courts; the other already has a binding result. But both hit the same nerve:

**In many companies' playbooks, pre-IPO equity is to be held tightly in "insider" hands. The more vested and valuable you are, the more likely you become the one who "needs handling."**

This isn't a conspiracy theory. It's a mechanism with its own internal logic. Let's take it apart.

---

## 3. Why you "have equity" but can't necessarily "cash it out"

The one thing to remember: **"I have options" and "I can cash them out" are two completely different things.**

Before an IPO, your options are mostly not company stock registered in your name. They sit in a **holding vehicle** (typically a limited partnership). You are a **Limited Partner (LP)**; the **General Partner (GP)** — the one who actually decides — is usually the boss or an entity he controls.

The structure itself is a legitimate compliance tool. But it inherently creates three asymmetries you must be aware of:

**First, pricing is asymmetric.** Private shares have no market price. If the company buys back your interest, how is "fair value" set? In practice, the valuation report is commissioned and paid for by the company; the employee has almost no leverage.

**Second, dilution is asymmetric.** Capital increases, share adjustments, and transfers at the vehicle level are decided by the GP. Whether each LP must individually consent depends on how the partnership agreement is written — and that agreement is usually drafted by the company. One core element of Song Tao's complaint is precisely his claim that the vehicle's shares were unilaterally transferred and diluted (his claim; Dobot denies it).

**Third, information is asymmetric.** What you sign, when, which version binds, whether you hold a copy — the initiative is mostly with the company. Many employees first seriously study what they signed only on the day they settle up at exit.

Put the three together and the conclusion is clear: **before an IPO, what your equity is worth and whether you can take it depends far less on the number in the contract than on the other side's willingness — and on the evidence you kept.**

---

## 4. Don't assume time-based vesting keeps you safe

Many engineers have a naive comfort: "My contract says it vests over three or four tranches; I just wait it out and it's mine."

Lovely in theory. In reality there are at least three common "soft plays":

- **Stall.** They don't process the paperwork when it vests; if you don't push, it hangs.
- **Re-paper.** Under legitimate-sounding banners like "standardizing the holding vehicle" or "IPO compliance," you're asked to sign a new agreement — and in it, already-vested or soon-to-vest units may be re-locked until after the IPO. Standardization in name, a fresh lock in effect.
- **Haggle.** Even if they agree to buy back, the price is set by the company's side.

The one to watch most is the fourth — **signing away rights without fully realizing it.**

Companies generate mountains of documents. Often a secretary hands you only the last signature page — "board resolution, just a formality" — and you sign. Who reads word for word? Yet inside those "formalities" can hide clauses that change lock-up terms or confirm a "voluntary waiver."

**The plain bottom line: if a document tied to your net worth is one the other side won't even give you a copy of, it's probably not "just a formality."**

## 5. How a founder's mindset changes, step by step

Beyond mechanism, there's human nature. Many who've lived it will tell you: the boss wasn't like this at the start — on the contrary, he was so good to you it was hard to refuse.

**Before a big round, the founder is often the humblest person in the company.** Short on money, people, and credibility, you're his bargaining chip. At this stage he can be extraordinarily good to you: courting you, calling you "brother," offering equity when salary talks stall, painting a future that sets your blood on fire. In his public account, Song Tao says he gave up a million-yuan salary at Huawei because a "senior schoolmate" coaxed him, heart and soul, into Dobot (his own telling). I've written before about a similar first-hand experience: in the early days the boss was frugal, staying in 200-yuan-a-night hotels on business trips, grinding late in the office with everyone, and even the boss's own requests were discussed as equals.

Were those promises sincere? I lean toward yes — at least in the moment they were made. **The issue isn't sincerity; it's that the promise cost almost nothing at the time.** With the company worth nothing, a point of equity was just a number on paper; generosity was effortless.

**After a big round, the balance tilts.** The money lands, the valuation climbs, and yesterday's "numbers on paper" carry, for the first time, a heart-stopping price. A quiet re-accounting happens in the founder's head: he remembers the salary you joined on and the work you did back then; he sees the astronomical figure your stake now converts to at the new valuation. Set side by side, one thought becomes almost unavoidable — **"I gave away too much."**

Then behavior starts to change, along a strikingly familiar path:

- **Bring in "stronger people."** High-paid new executives are hired from big firms, ostensibly to fill gaps, with the practical effect of diluting the old guard's power and voice. The once-equal discussions in the meeting room slide into "alignment," "enablement," and unspoken flattery.
- **Re-rank identities.** The comrades who paved the roads and built the walls become, in the new corporate narrative, "historical contributors" — which translates to: low-ROI assets.
- **"Handle legacy issues."** The generous promises of the past are now costs on the books, "uncertainties" ahead of the IPO — hence all the "standardizing," "adjusting," and "re-signing."

I don't think this is one founder's unique corruption; it's more like a structural temptation: **when a promise goes from costing nothing to being worth a fortune, the character required to honor it is far greater than the character it took to make it.** Most people never face that test in their lives, and you can't know in advance whether your boss will pass it.

So the takeaway for working people is simple: **don't use the pre-funding founder to predict the post-funding one.** What's really worth looking at is his track record when real money is on the table — which is exactly why the next section's two positive examples matter.

## 6. The other direction: what a good boss looks like

After all this "pitfall" talk you might feel bleak. So let me be clear: **the problem isn't equity incentives themselves — it's that some people use them badly.** Bosses and companies who do it well exist, and are worth keeping as a reference frame when you choose where to work.

**Example 1: ByteDance — Guo Yu.**

Guo Yu was an engineer at ByteDance, not formally trained, self-taught. He worked there about six years, rose to senior technical expert, and his options rose several hundredfold with the company's valuation — worth over a hundred million. In 2020, at 28, he announced his "retirement" to live the life he wanted.

It became famous not just for the wish-fulfillment of "engineer reaches financial freedom," but for something scarce behind it: **the company actually let an ordinary engineer turn paper options into money in his pocket.** ByteDance is also known for another practice — **periodically buying back veteran employees' shares while still private, to provide liquidity.** In other words, it didn't fob employees off with "we're not public, so the options can't be cashed" — it actively turned paper wealth into real money.

**Example 2: MiniMax — Yan Junjie.**

In July 2026, Yan Junjie, founder of the large-model company MiniMax, sent an all-hands letter announcing that, until the company achieves AGI, he will no longer draw a salary; and over the next four years, **he will take 4% of his personal shareholding to reward the team that has long fought alongside the company, and 1% to support the open-source community.**

Notably, the backdrop for this letter was the first large unlock after MiniMax's IPO, when the stock was visibly volatile. **While others think about how to cash out at the unlock, he thought about how to give more from his own pocket to the team.** The contrast speaks for itself.

I have no interest in canonizing anyone; there's no pure charity in business. But these two examples at least offer a clear yardstick:

> **A good equity culture treats the original promise as a debt to be repaid; a bad one treats it as baggage to shed before the IPO.**

Your job is to tell, as early as possible — at hiring, at signing, in watching what the boss says and does — which kind you're in.

## 7. Across the Pacific: how Silicon Valley handles this

After the domestic cases, a natural question: is this a universal startup disease, or something particular to us? The answer: **human nature is the same everywhere, but the "default" of the rules is not.**

**Difference one: vested means yours.**

The Valley standard is "4-year vesting with a 1-year cliff": after one year you get a quarter, then it vests monthly. The key point — **the vested portion, under their rules, is your property, not the boss's favor.** Leaving and taking it is the default; you need no one's "mercy," and there is no "principle" of "forfeit everything if you leave before the IPO."

The most extreme example is Ilya Sutskever. He's an OpenAI co-founder and chief scientist — and the man who, in the late-2023 board turmoil, led the vote to remove CEO Sam Altman. By some companies' playbook, wouldn't such a "ringleader" have his equity scrubbed to nothing on the way out? Reality: he left in May 2024 and took his shares untouched, founding his own company weeks later; in 2026 court filings, he disclosed his OpenAI stake to be worth about **$7 billion**.

**Compare: at some companies, an employee gets "handled" just for telling the truth; over there, the man who led the vote to fire the CEO keeps his equity on the way out.** That's the gap in the "default."

**Difference two: when something goes wrong, they fix it fast.**

Don't misunderstand — I'm not saying the Valley is heaven. In May 2024, OpenAI itself had a scandal: reporting revealed its exit paperwork hid a clause under which declining a non-disparagement agreement could cost you your *vested* equity — one researcher who refused to sign faced forfeiting roughly $2 million. See? The urge to "reclaim employees' earned equity" tempts bosses everywhere.

But the point is what happened next: after the reporting, an uproar; Altman apologized publicly, calling it one of the few times he'd been genuinely embarrassed running OpenAI, and left the widely-quoted line — **"vested equity is vested equity, full stop."** The clause was deleted within days, and the company pledged never to claw anyone's equity back.

Same urge to "reclaim" — the difference isn't in the courts (in the Chen Hao case, our courts likewise stood firmly on the worker's side); the difference is in corporate self-awareness: over there, peer consensus and public pressure make a company correct itself in days; while some companies would rather make an employee run the full multi-year gauntlet than admit early to a bill they always owed.

**Difference three: you don't have to wait for the IPO to cash out.**

SpaceX runs a tender offer roughly every six months, letting employees sell part of their vested shares for cash; Stripe and OpenAI have done large-scale versions, letting employees cash out at the latest valuation before going public. **The IPO isn't the only door to liquidity, and "unlisted = worthless paper" simply doesn't hold in that system.** ByteDance's periodic buybacks, mentioned earlier, are learning from exactly this.

Of course, the Valley has its own pitfalls — the classic is the "90-day exercise window": after leaving you must pay to exercise within 90 days or the options expire, trapping many "at their desks." But in recent years Pinterest, Coinbase and others have extended the window to 7–10 years; the industry is filling the pits bit by bit.

So the conclusion of this section isn't "the foreign moon is rounder," but a cooler line: **the soul of an equity program isn't in the contract text — it's in the company's default. "Vested is yours" is, at a well-governed company, a starting point written on day one; at a dysfunctional one, it's something an employee must go to great lengths to win.**

## 8. A few self-checks for engineers

You don't need to become a legal expert, but the following are cheap and can save you at the critical moment:

1. **Read every agreement word for word before signing** — especially lock-up, exit/buyback, and dispute-resolution clauses.
2. **Keep a copy of every document you sign.** No copy, no signature — that's a floor, not fussiness.
3. Periodically check your company's holding vehicle in public corporate registries; if the structure or shares change in ways you didn't know about, raise your guard.
4. The moment you see **"re-lock," "transfer into the holding platform," "supplemental agreement," "voluntary waiver,"** wake up: it's a signal, not a formality.
5. **Leave a paper trail.** Put important communications in email/writing. Much of what's done leaves traces, and traces are your trump card when it matters — both Song Tao and Chen Hao could speak up precisely because of what they'd kept on the record.
6. **Spot trouble early, negotiate early, and cut losses early if it stalls.** Hesitation often costs more than the equity itself.

---

## 9. In closing

Song Tao's rights and wrongs are for regulators and the law. But the crack he pried open — **how a company, on the eve of going public, treats the very people who walked the whole road with it** — is worth a hard look from everyone holding options.

Keeping your eyes open doesn't mean assuming the worst of every boss. Quite the opposite: precisely because a ByteDance-for-Guo-Yu and a MiniMax-under-Yan-Junjie really exist, you should have the discernment — **to recognize the good companies and dodge the bad signals.**

This isn't distrust. It's the most basic clarity between adults.

Today it's Song Tao; tomorrow it could be you, or me. May the day you finally cash out, you hold real money — not a piece of paper you have to sue to redeem.
