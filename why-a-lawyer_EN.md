# After 35, we need to know a lawyer

> This piece is about **why** — why finding a lawyer shouldn't wait until something has already gone wrong.

There's a saying in China: there are three kinds of people everyone should know — a teacher, a doctor, and a lawyer.

I'd heard it for years. Only recently did I understand what it actually means: it's a statement about life after 35.

---

## 1. Why 35

Before 35, what we compete on is craft. Get good, chase the thing, fly forward.

After 35, it changes. Those three kinds of people map onto three things that start accumulating in our hands:

- Children going to school — that needs a teacher.
- Ageing parents, young kids, a body that needs more attention than it used to — that needs a doctor.
- A title, equity, contracts — and the risks attached to those sit largely **outside** our professional competence. That needs a lawyer.

Most people attend to the first two without being told. The third is the one almost everyone leaves until after something has gone wrong.

And "gone wrong" isn't far away. The moments that call for a lawyer look like this: the equity or options you were promised at hire, quietly reworked by the time they're supposed to vest; how much severance you're actually owed when you're laid off, and that non-compete you signed without reading; a prenup, whose name goes on the deed, custody in a divorce; the "high-yield wealth product" someone talked your parents into. Even the person you were close to — money you transferred because you were asked to, a document you signed on their behalf. When it ends, none of it comes back.

So after 35, **the investment with the best return is the one you make in yourself** — and not only in professional skills. Learn from people who have seen parts of the world you haven't: from teachers, from doctors, from lawyers. What grows out of that time and money is judgement. And life after 35 is largely a contest of judgement.

This isn't cynicism. There's a bleak little pattern worth naming: **the people looking to take advantage are the ones who study the law hardest.** Whoever is sliding the contract across the table has read every clause. Whoever is setting the trap knows exactly which money can't be clawed back and which signature can't be undone.

Rules exist to protect people who play straight. Instead the people who use them best are the ones doing the calculating, and the people who never study them are the straight players. That's backwards.

Someone who works in the legal industry told me: *"Ordinary people in China avoid contact with lawyers. They think making contact invites trouble."*

We're still squeamish about talking to a lawyer at all. The other side wore out their copy of the statute years ago.

And disputes follow a cruel rhythm: they surface when a relationship turns bad, but the mine was laid when the relationship was at its best — back when we trusted, weren't watching, and signed whatever was put in front of us. Once we're on guard, nothing can be planted.

When someone is being better to us than we expected, that's often exactly the moment to keep a small part of the mind awake.

Which means the defences can only be built in ordinary time.

There are two kinds of relationship we can have with a lawyer, and they run in parallel:

- **Client and counsel** — something goes wrong, we hire them, they litigate and recover what can be recovered.
- **Friend and adviser** — an ongoing acquaintance, someone who flags things: who to be careful with, which contract not to sign, which move will create a problem later.

---

## 2. What we're looking for isn't only a lawyer — it's an adviser

Every one of us lives inside some kind of cage. Clauses we can't see, buried in contracts. Bosses who help themselves to our time. Property, markets, dowries, taking their turns.

These rules aren't unknowable. It's just that nobody ever explained them to us. The people who do know them assume we don't — and prefer it that way.

But I'm not saying this to be bleak.

Romain Rolland has a line that gets quoted endlessly:

> *"There is only one heroism in the world: to see the world as it is, and to love it."*

It usually gets quoted from the middle — only the second half survives. *Love the world.*

But the real precondition is the first half: **see it as it is.** Loving it without seeing it isn't heroism, it's luck. And luck runs out.

Seeing it as it is, though, isn't something a life that runs between two fixed points can give us. Office to home, home to office, the same handful of people, the same scripts, the same story about how things work. We think we're living; mostly we're running laps inside rules someone else wrote.

And on the day something breaks, we discover how much of the world's actual machinery we'd never looked at.

Lawyers have seen a great deal of what people are capable of, and a great deal of how things actually run. They can read a workplace for us, read a boss, tell us who is worth being careful with, head things off early. **Keeping most problems away from a courtroom is the most valuable thing a lawyer does.**

So we're not only looking for a lawyer. We're looking for an adviser.

## 3. My most expensive lesson — it cost a year

When the trouble started, my first instinct was to call the one lawyer I knew: a university classmate, who works mostly in criminal defence and construction disputes. He was the only lawyer I knew.

As a friend, he was attentive and generous. He wouldn't take my money — I offered, and he refused.

His advice was to wait: let the other side move first. He was confident a particular thing "couldn't happen."

It happened. The other side executed cleanly, one step after another.

I missed the negotiating window, lost the better part of a year, and came close to running into a hard limitation deadline.

**I knew exactly one lawyer, so I called him. That was a serious mistake.**

Where's the mistake? **Lawyers specialise, the way doctors do.**

When we're ill we know to book the right department; nobody takes chest pain to an orthopaedist. But when we look for a lawyer, most people ask only "are you a lawyer?" — not "what kind of cases do you actually do?" My classmate's home turf was elsewhere. My problem wasn't on his track. He gave it everything he had, but judgement from an adjacent field is still judgement from an adjacent field.

That's the principle. What follows is what I picked up along the way: what to look for, how specialisation actually works, and where to look.

---

## 4. Three principles

### One: study your own case

The most immediate benefit of understanding your own case is being able to explain it quickly and completely. That saves the lawyer's time and helps them reach an accurate read faster — what might have taken several consultations can take one. The saving is money too.

And how you tell it shapes the read. The same set of facts, framed differently, can produce genuinely different assessments. A client's own analysis can move a lawyer's view of the case — and can move how much they're willing to put into it.

I put together a set of materials once. One lawyer went through it and moved the strategy session forward — from the following week to that Sunday evening. The better prepared we are, the more they invest, and the more confidence they have in the case. That confidence eventually turns into odds.

### Two: AI first

Anything solvable with a search engine or an AI, solve it first. What's left over is what the lawyer is for — the part the AI can't cover.

This isn't only about saving money. Walking in with the basic concepts and the rough procedure already understood means asking sharper questions and actually following the answer. And remember: every real decision in a case — fight or settle, sign or don't — is ours to make. How well we make those calls depends on how much law we understand.

### Three: never get free work out of a lawyer

Treat their time as the asset it is. Bluntly: there are a lot of people right now trying to get legal work for free, and lawyers know precisely who is doing it. Time is money. It comes back around, with interest.

I learned this one personally. That university classmate wouldn't take payment — I offered, he declined. It sounds like an enormous favour. In practice it cost us both. He wasn't being paid, so the attention he could reasonably give was limited. And every time I wanted to push, or ask for more, I hesitated first: *I'm not even paying him.* And so it drifted, politely, week after week.

So even with someone you know, even when they're embarrassed to charge — if they spent time on a consultation, pay for the consultation. Paying isn't politeness. It's treating their expertise as expertise. It also buys us the standing to ask for things without flinching. Take their time seriously and they'll take the matter seriously.

## 5. "Specialisation" goes two layers deeper

The conclusion I paid a year for goes deeper than "book the right department."

Someone in the industry gave me a number: among lawyers with ten or twenty years of practice in one first-tier city, **around 80% built their careers on real estate and construction work.** That work has thinned out, so naturally they take other things. They can certainly take our case — but their grasp of the statute, and of how the local courts actually lean on this type of dispute, may not be precise. And that lean is often what decides it. A judge in the room is weighing which advocate's position is better grounded and more credible. That has nothing to do with years of practice and everything to do with how many of *this* kind of case they've run. When the advocate isn't of that field, the position simply doesn't carry the same weight.

And lawyers specialise by region as well as by subject. Even the "N+1" severance formula in employment law is applied differently in Beijing, Shanghai and Guangdong — *"even an employment specialist will get it wrong across regions."*

So however you find a lawyer, open with two questions: **What kind of cases do you mainly do? And mainly where?**

Two more assumptions worth dropping:

- **More years ≠ more reliable.** Longevity says they've lasted. It doesn't say they're fast on our particular track.
- **A big firm ≠ a guarantee.** The firm signs the contract; the person doing the work may not be the person in the room. Fronting a senior name and sending a substitute to a hearing are both ordinary industry practice. So ask: will you personally handle this, or the team? Who drafts? Who appears?

## 6. Two paths — pick one

There are more than two ways to find a lawyer: introductions, approaching firms directly, paid online consultations, looking up a lawyer's actual track record in the national judgments database. The trade-offs of each are in the [handbook](./find-a-lawyer.md).

Here I'll cover the two most common situations.

### Path one: no lawsuit, no dispute, nothing wrong right now

Which makes this the good time to prepare.

Because on the day it does go wrong, a person is making the single most important decision of the whole affair in the state of least composure and worst judgement. And if there's exactly one lawyer in the contacts list, there's nothing to compare against and nothing to choose between. That's how my own mistake happened.

Go meet a few lawyers now.

People nearby, alumni, anyone who came out of a law faculty — nearly every university has one, and an introduction is usually one degree away. Follow a few firms or legal writers; firms run free public talks and seminars fairly often, and their accounts are where you sign up. Go to one. Who's speaking and what they specialise in is immediately obvious, and asking a couple of questions afterwards is far more natural than adding a stranger cold.

Friendships aren't made in a day. From acquaintance to familiarity to trust usually takes more than a year — which is exactly the argument for stepping outside our own small circle now rather than later.

And honestly, the value of these friendships goes well beyond "someone to call in an emergency." They've seen a different slice of the world. One conversation and our understanding of contracts, of people, of how an industry really operates gains a layer. "Seeing things as they are" often starts with exactly this kind of friend.

How close is close enough? Roughly: close enough to send over a contract you're unsure about. And when they read it and give you an analysis — pay the consultation fee. This isn't social reciprocity, it's ordinary professional service. Half an hour on a contract can remove three years of litigation.

But hold on to the earlier lesson: **a lawyer friend's value is in ordinary time** — reading contracts, flagging things, and when something serious happens, introducing us to the person who actually fits. When it is serious, don't hand the whole case to them because they're a friend. Where the specialisation doesn't match, no amount of goodwill closes the gap.

If there's genuinely no time to build friendships slowly, there's a more direct route: pay for a formal consultation, and prioritise the mentor type.

What's a mentor type? They don't just answer "can this be sued." They ask back: **what is it you actually want?** They don't just read the contract in front of them — they'll tell you where this industry usually sets its traps.

They're expensive. What they're selling is judgement. And judgement keeps paying out for the rest of your life.

### Path two: something has already happened, and there's no lawyer yet

Don't sign an engagement letter yet. First, judge one thing: how complex is this?

For ordinary matters — small claims, a routine employment arbitration — a lawyer from a personal introduction is generally sufficient. This is where those friendships earn out.

But if the case is complex, or the amount is large, see several and compare. Don't stop at the first person who says *leave it to me*. Because the central difficulty of a complex case is something I said to my wife at the time, and it holds up as a description: **"Every lawyer says he can do it, and I genuinely can't tell."** Seeing several is how you find where their opinions diverge — and the divergence is usually the real difficulty in the case.

Before comparing, clear one authenticity check. There are a large number of "legal consulting companies" online passing themselves off as law firms, running ads with lines like *"your case is simple, we'll close it in 7 to 14 days."* Take thirty seconds and look the person up in the national lawyer credentials registry. Also: legitimate legal fees go to the firm's corporate account. Anyone asking for a transfer to a personal WeChat or Alipay — end the conversation.

I also went down a road few people take, offered here as one data point: finding a **"lawyer broker."**

First, about those quotation marks. There is no such job title in China; you won't find it on any recruitment site. What I mean is people who know the industry and are relatively neutral — legal writers with a following, the people behind a firm's public account, more junior lawyers, or others working in and around the legal industry.

Consider: insurance has brokers. Performers have agents. Even buying an apartment comes with someone to help compare. Yet in the one transaction with the highest decision cost of all, most people choose blind. Someone was always going to fill that gap.

What these people have in common is that they're *inside* the circle. They know who is genuinely good at what, whether a particular lawyer's plate is full this month, even who has run a similar matter in front of which court.

The value is neutrality:

- An introduction from someone you know has too short a radius.
- Approach a firm directly and they will tell you they're a fit.
- Only a knowledgeable, neutral third party has any chance of closing that information gap.

But to be clear: this is not a mature channel. No licensing, no standards, and what you get depends heavily on luck. So it's *worth trying*, not *worth relying on*.

I got lucky. Here's how it went.

**How I found one.** I came across someone doing this work through a public account. After adding her, I skipped the small talk entirely and laid out the situation: the type of dispute, that the amount wasn't small, that I'd already spoken to several lawyers and couldn't tell them apart.

Her answer made me think the road might go somewhere. She said a lot of lawyers just want the work, and will take it whether or not it fits; her group screens what they take, because they want depth.

> *"This era isn't short of information. What it's short of is the ability to combine past experience with an industry-insider's view and help a client actually decide. Get the person right and the solution is probably right too."*

The next day she came to meet us, bringing a lawyer who worked in the relevant area. When the conversation turned to what role they'd be playing, she said:

> **"We'll be your advisers."**

Anyone can say something that lands well, and we didn't decide on the strength of one sentence — we still compared several more lawyers and ran background checks before settling. What made me remember the line was that what she did kept matching it.

**What does "adviser" actually mean? An example.** During that conversation I mentioned something I'd watched happen: a company owner sitting on a supplier's payment. Goods delivered and accepted, payment date written into the contract in black and white, and simply not paid.

She heard it out and described how this normally goes, in an entirely ordinary tone. From the supplier deciding to act to actually filing, several months. Filing, scheduling, hearing — a year. The other side's logic is to pay as late as possible and as little as possible; drag it out until the supplier's cash flow hurts and they'll accept a discount. When it truly can't be dragged further, the court mediates and the claim gets withdrawn. *"Because breaking the rules is cheap, and enforcing your rights is expensive."*

What actually happened afterwards tracked her description almost exactly.

There's nothing mystical about that. She has seen this kind of thing constantly at work. What we run into for the first time is their ordinary Tuesday. **An adviser is simply someone with a large enough sample size.**

**Be careful here too.** Plenty of marketing intermediaries also claim to match you with a lawyer, while actually living on referral kickbacks. Three questions separate them:

1. **Did they spend time asking about the details of the matter?** Anyone who asks only "do you want a lawyer" and immediately pushes a name is in sales.
2. **Will they say something against their own interest?** Things like "nobody wins this one" or "you don't need a lawyer for this." Only someone willing to say that is on your side.
3. **Do they only ever recommend one firm?** If so, they're most likely that firm's unofficial sales channel.

And one hard line: **a broker is a staff officer, not the decision-maker.** They can shortlist, compare prices and run background checks — but meeting the lawyer and signing the engagement has to be us. Ask the important questions to the lawyer directly, never through an intermediary.

---

None of this is urgent today. But whenever there's a free moment, two small things: scroll the contacts list and think about whether anyone there practises law; and follow a legal account or two. Either one counts as having started.

## Finally, something else

Wang Yangming, the Ming-dynasty philosopher, wrote: *"A person must be polished by real affairs before they can stand firm."* Judgement can only be ground out on actual events. I'd read plenty of avoid-the-pitfalls guides. It took losing something to understand that having read and having understood are different states.

This isn't gratitude for harm. Harm is harm. It's only that since the thing happened, better it grow into part of you than sit on top of you as a stone.

Now Romain Rolland's line can be finished: *"There is only one heroism in the world: to see the world as it is, and to love it."* The first half takes experience. The second takes courage. This piece can only help with the first.

One last thing. We learn this, and find lawyers, not to push anyone around — but to protect ourselves.

Remember *"breaking the rules is cheap, enforcing your rights is expensive"*? The statutes are public. The courts are open to everyone. And yet the people who use them fluently tend to be the ones who can afford a legal team — **the very complexity of the rules is already working for the strong.** So for ordinary people, knowing a little law and knowing a few lawyers isn't refinement. It's self-defence.

What this whole piece is trying to say fits in one sentence: **the law is supposed to be an ordinary person's weapon, not a moat for the wealthy and powerful.** We shouldn't have to become powerful before we qualify for protection. We only need to learn to reach it before something goes wrong.

And the more of us who understand a little law, the more expensive it becomes to take advantage of us. When enough ordinary people know the law and can use it, that arithmetic starts to run the other way.

The plainest version of what we call a society under rule of law is probably just this — not only complete statutes and open courts, but ordinary people, one by one, who understand them.

**A society where everyone understands the law will be fairer and more just. It will simply be a better society.**
